Real-time geofencing pipeline — ~1M GPS pings a minute

The distributed system I'm proudest of: a pipeline that ingests a firehose of vehicle GPS and turns it into ground truth — where a shuttle is, whether it's on its intended route, and whether employees reached safely. Across ~100K devices and ~100K geofences, without melting the fleet backend.

What it powers

It's the backbone under several products, not a single feature: live tracking for admins (is a vehicle actually running on its intended route?), safety / verified-reach tracking (did employees get where they were going?), and live ETAs for riders. ETA is just one consumer among several — every downstream that needs "where is this vehicle, right now, relative to a boundary" sits on top of it.

The problem

The legacy path recomputed every rider's ETA on every GPS ping — iterating route stops and running a road-network distance calc per stop. At ~1M pings/minute from ~100K devices against ~100K active geofences, ETA p95 had climbed to ~2.5s, and during the 8:30–10:00 AM peak ETAs lagged reality by 30–60s — riders saw "5 min away" while the shuttle was already at the gate, causing missed boardings.

The insight

Out of ~1M pings a minute, only a few hundred are state-changing — a vehicle actually entering or leaving a fence. Everything else is just movement. So I built the pipeline around per-transition event handling instead of per-ping recomputation: the expensive work happens only when a fence boundary is crossed. The rest of the firehose just updates position in a purpose-built geospatial store that does the boundary math — and every consumer (tracking, safety, ETA) reads off those transitions.

▸ The pipeline, end to end
  1. Ingest GPS pings from those devices. Each vehicle unit and driver phone holds an open WebSocket to a SocketCluster layer and streams position packets. To avoid ~100K open sockets on my own service, the Geofencing Service subscribes at BUID (per-tenant) granularity — a few hundred logical subscriptions — while SocketCluster does the device-side fan-in.
  2. Write Position lands in the geospatial store. For each ping, the Geofencing Service writes the new position into Tile38 over its command protocol — directly, not through Kafka. This is the latency-sensitive hot path, so it stays as short as possible.
  3. Evaluate Tile38 does the geofence math. On every position update it evaluates the point against ~100K active geofences and decides whether a stop-fence entry or exit just happened. The vast majority of pings cross no boundary and produce nothing further — that silence is the whole win.
  4. Transition Only entries/exits fan out. On a transition, Tile38 fires a hook onto a Kafka callback topic; the Geofencing Service (its sole consumer) transforms it and emits a clean fence-transition event onto a downstream Kafka topic — a few hundred events a minute, not a million.
  5. Consume Many consumers read the same transitions. Client services consume the transition topic and each does its own thing: admin live-tracking checks whether a vehicle is on its intended route, safety tracking confirms employees reached their destination, and rider ETAs update and push at per-vehicle granularity (riders track their shuttle, not a stop), plus polling REST for clients that prefer it. Reliable stop-arrival events also drive trip-lifecycle automation (no more manual "arrived" tapping).

Why BUID-granularity subscription

Subscribing per-device would mean ~100K open sockets on the Geofencing Service — kernel-level FD/ephemeral-port/TCP tuning and a big scale-up. Subscribing at BUID granularity collapses that to a few hundred connections with no OS tuning. SocketCluster owns the device-side fan-in; the service does per-BUID logical processing. It's a partition-by-tenant move on the connection axis.

Why Kafka sits where it does

Writes (device → Tile38) are latency-sensitive and don't need replay → direct path, no Kafka. Tile38 callbacks are bursty → buffer them through Kafka with the service as sole consumer. Downstream fan-out has many consumers and wants replay → Kafka again. The placement is deliberately asymmetric, not uniform.

Shipping it without a bad night

I ran the new pipeline in shadow for ~3 weeks — both paths computed ETAs, only the legacy one answered queries — with a daily diff. Cutover was gated: within ±15s of legacy on 98%+ of stops, at a quarter of the latency, sustained for five consecutive days — then a controlled 5% → 25% → 100% ramp over a week. One post-cutover incident (a Tile38 master OOM after a new customer pushed fence-count past headroom) failed over in ~15s with no rider impact, and put fence-count on the capacity dashboard.

What I'd revisit: fence registration is a dual-write — the service writes both Tile38 (active-fence index) and a relational DB (fence metadata), so a crash between the two leaves them inconsistent. I'd make the DB authoritative and treat Tile38 as a derived projection, rebuilt via a transactional outbox — shortening failover recovery and unlocking sharded, multi-region Tile38. I'd also split ingest from projection into two services along the Kafka callback boundary so they scale independently.
